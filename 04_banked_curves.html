Banked Curves & Circular Motion Applications

1. Why bank a curve?

A banked road tilts so that the normal force has a horizontal component pointing toward the center. That component can provide some or all of the centripetal force.

2. Ideal frictionless banked curve

For a car moving at the design speed with no friction required:

N cosθ = mg
N sinθ = mv²/r

Divide the equations:

tanθ = v²/(rg)
θ = tan⁻¹(v²/rg)

Here θ is measured from the horizontal road surface. Use this relationship only for the ideal frictionless design-speed case.

3. What if friction is present?

Friction may point up or down the bank depending on whether the car tends to slide down or up the bank. Draw the free-body diagram carefully and resolve the normal and friction forces into horizontal and vertical components. Do not memorize one universal friction direction.

4. Problem-solving sequence

  1. Draw mg vertically downward.
  2. Draw N perpendicular to the banked surface.
  3. If included, draw friction parallel to the surface and determine its direction from the likely slipping tendency.
  4. Set vertical acceleration to zero if the car stays at constant height.
  5. Set net inward horizontal force equal to mv²/r.

5. Packet connection

Your circular-motion solutions packet includes a banked-curve problem asking for the angle of a curve given its radius and speed. This is the ideal banked-curve setup: calculate tanθ = v²/(rg), then take inverse tangent.

6. Common errors

  • Using sin and cos on the wrong components. With θ measured from horizontal, N cosθ is vertical and N sinθ is horizontal.
  • Forgetting the calculator must be in degree mode if the answer is requested in degrees.
  • Using diameter instead of radius.
  • Applying the frictionless formula when the question explicitly requires friction.
